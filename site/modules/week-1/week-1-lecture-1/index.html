Content

Video 1: From answers to actions

Duration: approximately 10 minutes Objectives: LO1, LO2 Purpose: Give learners a reliable mental model for distinguishing a chatbot response from an agent workflow.

A chatbot usually waits for a prompt and returns a response. An agentic system can use that response as one step in a larger loop: interpret a goal, choose an action, use a tool, inspect the result, and continue or stop. The distinction is about what the system is allowed and designed to do, not whether it sounds conversational. Many everyday products combine these patterns.

The video uses a family-calendar example to show how goal, model, tools, instructions, and human approval shape the loop. Learners practice identifying the difference between suggesting a meeting time and actually sending invitations. The key takeaway is that every action comes from a designed boundary, and a person should decide where that boundary belongs.

Lecture script

Video 1 script — From answers to actions

[Opening — show a simple family calendar with a school concert and two work commitments.]

If you have used a chatbot, you know the basic exchange: you ask a question, it responds. You might ask, “What is a good way to plan a week with a school concert and two late meetings?” The chatbot can suggest a plan. It does not necessarily know your calendar, check whether a time is free, or make a change.

Now imagine a system that can read a calendar you choose, compare availability, draft a plan, and perhaps create an event. We have moved from generating an answer to taking steps. That is the useful distinction behind the word “agent.” An agent is a system that pursues a goal through a sequence of steps, often by selecting tools and using the results to decide what to do next.

The word can sound like a new kind of intelligence. It is more helpful to think of an agent as a workflow with a language model in the middle. The model interprets instructions and proposes what should happen next. The surrounding software decides which tools are available, what information is supplied, what actions are permitted, and when the process should stop.

Let’s walk through the calendar case. A parent says, “Find a time for the family to review next week, and prepare an invitation.” The system needs a goal: find a time and prepare a draft. It needs context: perhaps the calendars the parent selected, the family’s time zone, and a rule that school events take priority. It needs a tool: a calendar reader. It may use a second tool to create a draft invitation. It also needs instructions about what to do if calendars conflict.

The agent might read three calendars, identify Tuesday evening as open, draft an invitation, and show the result. Each step changes the information available for the next step. If it can only prepare a draft, the parent still reviews names, time, and wording before anyone receives it. If it can send the invitation, it has a more consequential capability. The experience may look equally conversational, but the authority is different.

[On screen: Goal → Context → Model proposes step → Tool acts → Result checked → Continue or stop.]

This loop is a practical mental model. The model does not magically see the world. It receives information through the harness: the instructions and software surrounding it. It cannot use a calendar unless a calendar tool is connected and permitted. And a tool result is not a guarantee that the task succeeded. A calendar may be stale, an event may be missing, or the wrong person may share a name.

It is also worth separating three things that products often blur together. First, a chatbot can produce a useful answer without taking actions. Second, an agentic workflow can choose among steps and tools, sometimes repeating the loop. Third, a human may supervise the workflow, approving some actions and checking others. The same assistant can switch between these modes depending on the feature and permission settings.

Here is a test you can use when a product calls itself an agent: What can it actually do? What information can it see? Does it merely propose a step, or can it carry that step out? Does it inspect the result? Can it stop and ask for help? These questions tell you more than the label.

Consider a school lunch plan. A chatbot could suggest five lunches from ingredients you list. An agent might read a grocery list, look up recipes, and add ingredients to a shopping cart. If it can place the order, it has crossed from preparation into a purchase. If it can message a teacher or edit a child’s account, the effects involve other people and may be hard to reverse. A useful workflow makes those boundaries visible.

More steps do not automatically mean more value. If the task is “give me three ideas,” an ordinary response may be the simpler and safer choice. An agent is useful when the task genuinely involves multiple sources or actions and the added coordination is worth the setup and review. The goal is not to automate everything. It is to reduce repetitive effort while keeping the important decision with the right person.

[Pause.] Think of a routine task you handle each week. What information does it need? What could a system safely prepare? What action should wait for your approval? You have just sketched the first boundary of an agent workflow.

We will build on this model next by looking more closely at tools, instructions, and the harness that connects them. For now, remember: an agent is not just a chatbot with a grander name. It is a system that can take steps toward a goal, and its real behavior depends on the capabilities and boundaries people design around it.

Let’s make the distinction more concrete with a second example. A parent wants a list of indoor activities for a rainy Saturday. A chatbot can suggest ideas from what it already knows. An agent could check current library hours, search a museum’s public calendar, compare those times with a family schedule, and prepare a short plan. Each tool call gives the workflow new information. If the library site is unavailable, the system should say so. It should not invent an open time just to finish the plan.

There is a small but important design question here: what is the system being asked to optimize? “Find something fun” could mean low cost, short travel, accessibility, a particular interest, or a quiet afternoon. The agent cannot know which tradeoff matters most unless the person supplies it. It can present a few options and explain the tradeoffs. Choosing what makes the day right for this family remains a human judgment.

The agent loop also helps explain why a tool result is not the same as success. A search tool may return a page, but the page could describe last year’s event. The system may correctly call the tool and still return an outdated plan. The check step asks whether the result answers the goal with current evidence. If the answer is no, the next step might be to search an official page, ask the parent, or stop.

A helpful first experiment can be done without connecting an account. Give an assistant a fictional schedule and ask it to produce a draft plan. Then check: did it preserve fixed commitments, show travel assumptions, and avoid making a booking? This lets you observe the reasoning and output before granting access to personal information or external services.

This is a general habit: separate capability from permission. A product may offer a calendar tool, but your task may need only a suggested time. A system may be able to browse, but your question may not require current facts. A capability is an option in the design, not an obligation to use it.

[Closing: From asking for an answer to designing a responsible path to action.]
