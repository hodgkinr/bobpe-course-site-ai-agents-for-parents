Video 5: Autonomy is a choice about authority

Duration: approximately 10 minutes Objectives: LO3, LO4, LO5 Purpose: Help learners match autonomy to impact, reversibility, uncertainty, and people affected.

Autonomy describes how much an agent can do without asking at each step. It should be selected for a specific task, not inferred from how capable the system appears. A permission ladder—from suggesting and drafting through changing, sending, and purchasing—helps make authority visible.

The lecture introduces a practical risk lens: impact, reversibility, uncertainty, privacy, and affected people. It uses household scheduling and health-related questions to show why some actions need direct human review or qualified professional input. Learners consider staged pilots, narrow access, time limits, and ways to revoke permissions.

Lecture script

Video 5 script — Autonomy is a choice about authority

[Opening: three buttons labeled Suggest, Prepare, Act—with “Act” requiring approval.]

When an AI system can take steps, we have to decide how much authority it should have. Some people describe this as autonomy. It is not a score for how smart the system is. It is a choice about what the system may do without asking a person at every step.

Picture a family assistant that helps plan a birthday party. At the lowest level, it suggests themes. At the next, it drafts invitations and a shopping list. It might then edit a shared calendar or send invitations. At a higher level, it could reserve a venue or place an order. Each step gives the system more ability to affect other people, spend money, or create commitments.

[On screen: Suggest → Draft → Change → Send or commit.]

This ladder is a useful starting point, but the right level depends on the task. Editing a private draft may be low impact. Reading a sensitive file may be high impact even though it does not change anything. So ask five practical questions: How large could the impact be? Can the action be undone? How uncertain is the information? What private data is involved? Who else is affected?

For a low-risk, reversible task—say, sorting a list of fictional party themes—the system can probably act with little oversight. For a calendar draft, you might review before changing the shared schedule. For invitations, check recipients and wording before sending. For a purchase or venue booking, review the details and price before committing. For a decision about a child’s medical care, an AI agent should not replace a qualified clinician or emergency service. It can help prepare questions or organize information, but decisions and care belong with appropriate professionals and caregivers.

Oversight works best when it is placed at a meaningful boundary. If you approve every tiny formatting choice, you may be overwhelmed and miss the important decision. If you approve nothing, a mistake may reach the outside world before you see it. Decide which actions can happen automatically, which need a review of a draft, and which should remain entirely human.

A review is useful only if the person can make an informed decision. Show what the agent plans to do, why it chose that option, what sources it used, and what is uncertain. A button that says “Approve” without showing the recipient, amount, or changed record is not meaningful oversight. The reviewer also needs enough time and authority to say no.

It can help to start with a staged pilot. Begin with a task that is narrow, low consequence, and easy to check. For example, ask the system to draft a weekly meal plan from preferences you provide, without connecting shopping or payment. Check whether the suggestions fit. Note where it misunderstood. If the workflow is useful and predictable, you can decide whether to add a capability. You do not need to add it.

Permissions should be as narrow and temporary as practical. If the agent needs calendar access for a one-time plan, can you select a specific calendar or revoke access afterward? If it needs to read a document, can you provide that document instead of an entire drive? Access can be a door that remains open after the task is over, so notice how it is granted and removed.

There is also an important difference between a reversible suggestion and a real-world action. A system can suggest a school pickup change, but the parent should confirm the plan with the people who need to know. An automated message may be misread or sent to the wrong recipient. A safety plan should not depend on an agent noticing every exception. Keep human communication and emergency protocols clear.

When stakes rise or uncertainty remains, an agent should have a safe way to pause. That might be a clear instruction to ask, a tool restriction that blocks the action, or a human handoff. “Ask me if something seems important” is vague. “Do not send, submit, purchase, or change a child’s account; show me a draft and wait” is observable.

[Pause.] Think of a task you might delegate to a digital helper. What is the most it should be allowed to do in a first trial? Which step affects another person? What information would you need before approving that step?

The answer can change with evidence, but keep a simple rule in mind: match authority to the task’s impact and reversibility, then increase it only when the need is clear and the results are dependable. A system that can act quickly deserves boundaries designed before the action, not after a surprise.

In our final lecture, we will bring this together into a family practice for evaluating results, protecting information, and recovering when a workflow fails.

Let’s use a permission decision with no single perfect answer. A parent asks an agent to organize rides for a sports team. The agent can read the schedule and draft a ride chart. Should it also message families to ask who can drive? That message would reach other people and may reveal details about a child’s schedule. The parent could choose to review a draft first, share only the information families need, and send it through an approved team channel. The task can still benefit from automation, but the family decides how much authority is appropriate.

You can think of autonomy as a budget of authority. Spend it where the task is clear, low impact, and easy to check. Hold it back where the system is uncertain, where a choice affects someone else, or where an action is hard to undo. This framing keeps the conversation practical. You do not need to decide whether an agent is generally trustworthy; decide whether this particular action deserves to happen without review.

The amount of review should match the consequence. Reviewing every word of a private brainstorming list may waste attention. Checking an amount, recipient, date, account, or medical implication may be essential. If the system performs many routine steps, consider reviewing a sample while keeping all high-impact actions behind approval. The important thing is to know what is checked and why.

A staged pilot creates evidence. In week one, the helper might only suggest ride groupings from a fictional schedule. In week two, it could prepare a draft chart using selected real availability, with consent from the families involved. Only if the process is clear and useful would the parent consider sending a message—and that message still gets reviewed. Each stage is a separate choice, not an automatic path toward full automation.

Also ask how to revoke a permission. If an assistant connected to a calendar last month, does it still need that access today? If you cannot quickly find the setting or understand what remains stored, that is part of the system’s practical risk. A good autonomy decision includes the end of the task, not just its beginning.

[Closing: Autonomy is authority you choose—and responsibility you keep.]
