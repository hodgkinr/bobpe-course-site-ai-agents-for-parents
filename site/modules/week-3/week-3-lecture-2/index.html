Content

Video 6: Oversight, evaluation, and recovery

Duration: approximately 10 minutes Objectives: LO4, LO5 Purpose: Give learners a repeatable approach to evaluate an agent and respond when it fails.

Responsible use does not mean expecting perfection. It means deciding what evidence to check, how to handle uncertainty, and who is responsible for recovery. The lecture introduces a simple review cycle: define success, inspect sources and actions, check affected people and privacy, compare the result with the goal, and decide to accept, revise, retry, or stop.

Using a family schedule and a child-related health question, learners distinguish appropriate administrative help from decisions requiring qualified people. The lesson closes with a family agreement for private data, approval boundaries, escalation, and periodic review.

Lecture script

Video 6 script — Oversight, evaluation, and recovery

[Opening: a draft itinerary with one incorrect time highlighted.]

So far we have learned to describe an agent, shape its harness, and choose how much authority to grant it. Today we will ask a final question: how do we know whether the system did a good job, and what do we do when it did not?

A confident, neatly formatted answer is not enough. Evaluation begins with the original goal. If you asked for a schedule that avoids school conflicts, check the schedule against the school events. If you asked for sources, open them and see whether they support the summary. If the agent changed a calendar, inspect the actual calendar rather than relying on the sentence “Done.”

A practical review cycle has five parts. First, define what success means before the task begins. Second, inspect the evidence and assumptions. Third, check what actions occurred and who was affected. Fourth, compare the result with the goal. Finally, choose a next step: accept, revise, retry with a clearer request, or stop and use another approach.

[On screen: Goal → Evidence → Effects → Compare → Accept / Revise / Retry / Stop.]

Let’s use a family schedule. The agent was asked to find a time for a parent-teacher meeting and draft an invitation. It says Tuesday at 4:00 works. Check the calendars it used, confirm that the time zone is right, and inspect the invitation recipients. Did it create a draft or send a message? If the time is wrong, can you edit the draft before it affects someone? The review should match the action’s consequence.

Now consider a child who has a rash. A language model might summarize general information or help organize questions for a clinician. But it cannot examine the child, confirm a diagnosis, or replace emergency advice. If a situation may be urgent, use local emergency services or a qualified clinician. The system should not autonomously message a provider, change medication, or reassure a parent that the situation is safe based only on a generated response. This is a clear boundary: support preparation and understanding, while health decisions remain with responsible caregivers and qualified professionals.

The same care applies to privacy. Before using a service, consider what information it receives, what it stores, and whether family members understand the arrangement. Avoid sharing a child’s full name, account details, precise location, or sensitive records when the task can be done without them. Read the product’s current settings and terms; features and policies change, so do not rely on an old summary.

Oversight also includes knowing how to stop. Can you pause a running workflow? Revoke a tool connection? See what it already did? If an agent has sent an incorrect message, the immediate response may be to contact recipients and correct it. If it changed records, restore them from a known copy. If it disclosed information, follow the relevant service or organization’s process. A person remains responsible for noticing and addressing effects.

A good review is not the same as checking every token. Focus attention where it matters: uncertain sources, external actions, decisions involving children, sensitive data, and outcomes that are hard to reverse. For routine low-impact steps, occasional spot checks may be enough. For high-impact steps, do not delegate the decision to a quick scan of a generated summary.

It helps to agree on family rules before trying a tool. For example: “We do not put private school or health records into unapproved services. An assistant may draft messages but cannot send them. We review any purchase. We use a real person or qualified service for urgent health and safety questions. We can stop the pilot at any time.” The rules can be adjusted as children grow and family needs change.

[Pause.] If an agent made a mistake in a task you care about, what would you need to know first? What action would stop further effects? Who should be told? What would help you decide whether to try again or stop?

You do not have to trust an agent completely or reject it completely. You can evaluate one task in one setting, with clear boundaries. If the result is useful, keep the scope appropriate. If it fails, change the workflow or choose another tool. If the stakes exceed what you can supervise, do not automate that decision.

The course began with a simple idea: an agent is a system that can take steps toward a goal. We end with the companion idea: the people who set the goal, grant access, review effects, and take responsibility remain part of the system. Thoughtful use means keeping that role visible.

A review cycle can be different for a low-stakes task and a high-stakes one. For a draft birthday invitation, you might check the date, recipients, and tone. For a summary of public museum hours, you might verify the official page and notice when it was updated. For a question about a child’s symptoms, you should not rely on the agent to determine urgency; use an appropriate medical professional or emergency service. The same “check the output” instruction is not enough for every context.

When a system makes a mistake, first contain the effect. Pause additional actions, revoke an exposed permission if needed, and correct information for people who received it. Then investigate the cause. Was the source outdated? Did the instruction leave out an exception? Did the tool update the wrong account? Did a summary hide uncertainty? A useful improvement addresses the cause. Broadening access or asking the model to be more careful may not fix it.

Keep a simple record during a pilot: task, input used, result, what you checked, errors, and the next decision. This can be a few notes on paper. It helps separate a single lucky result from a repeatable process. If the task changes, reassess. An agent that drafts a family calendar from a small test may not be ready to coordinate a full school schedule with dozens of participants.

A family agreement is most useful when it is specific enough to guide a real moment. “Protect privacy” is a value; “do not paste school records or passwords into this tool” is a concrete rule. “Review important actions” is a goal; “a parent checks the exact recipients, date, and message before sending” tells people what to do. Include a way for a child or other family member to ask questions or say they are uncomfortable.

Finally, do not confuse a tool’s privacy controls with the appropriateness of the task. A service may offer deletion or restricted retention, but that does not mean every sensitive detail belongs there. Minimize information at the point of collection, then check settings and remove access when the task ends. This protects the family even when you cannot know every detail of how the underlying system works.

[Closing: Define success. Check evidence. Review consequences. Choose the next step.]
