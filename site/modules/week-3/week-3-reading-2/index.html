A Family Agreement for Trying an AI Agent

Learning objectives: Design a useful multi-step workflow with relevant information, narrow capabilities, checks, and stop conditions.; Match permissions and human review to an action’s impact, reversibility, uncertainty, privacy, and affected people.; Evaluate an agent’s result against evidence and the original goal, then choose to accept, revise, retry, or stop.

Content

A family agreement for trying an AI agent

Purpose: Turn the course’s design ideas into a practical, revisable family agreement. Reading time: 6–8 minutes Objectives: LO3, LO4, LO5

A family is considering an AI helper for household planning. One parent is excited to automate routines. Another is concerned about private information and mistakes. A teenager wants help organizing school and activities but does not want every choice monitored. A short agreement can make expectations concrete without requiring everyone to become an AI expert.

Agree on the purpose

Start with a small, useful task: perhaps draft a weekly meal plan from a list of preferences, or identify open times in a fictionalized copy of a schedule. Avoid a vague goal like “make family life easier.” State what the tool should produce and how the family will know it helped.

Make a data rule

Decide what may be shared and what stays out. Names, school records, passwords, exact addresses, health details, and private conversations deserve care. Often a general description is enough. For example, “a teenager with a 30-minute commute” may work when a full name and school address are unnecessary. Check current product settings and policies before connecting accounts.

Children should understand, in age-appropriate language, what data is being used and why. A parent’s permission does not make every disclosure necessary. Give family members a way to raise a concern or opt out of a particular task.

Draw an approval boundary

Write down what the tool may do on its own. It might suggest, summarize, or prepare drafts. Decide which actions always require review: sending messages, changing shared schedules, submitting schoolwork, purchasing, booking, or sharing a child’s information. Some decisions—especially emergency, health, safety, or legal matters—should go to qualified people and services.

Make the review practical. The person should see the exact action, affected account or recipient, relevant source, and cost or consequence. The process should pause until someone approves. If nobody is available, the agent should not treat silence as consent.

Decide how to evaluate a pilot

Run a short trial with a low-stakes task. Check outputs against the original information. Note errors, time saved, and whether family members found the process understandable. A pilot can end with “use it again,” “change the instructions,” “choose a different tool,” or “do this task ourselves.” All are reasonable outcomes.

Plan a stop and recovery route

Know how to pause the system, revoke access, and find out what it already did. Decide who will correct a mistaken message or calendar change. Keep a separate source of truth for important schedules and records. For urgent situations, use established human support rather than waiting for an AI workflow.

Revisit the agreement

Tools, settings, family needs, and school policies can change. Review the agreement after a new capability is connected, a child’s needs change, or an unexpected result occurs. Remove permissions that are no longer needed. A family agreement is a conversation, not a one-time legal form.

A practical agreement might read: “We will use the assistant only for meal-plan drafts this month. We will not share health, school, or account records. It may suggest and draft; an adult reviews before anything is sent, changed, booked, or purchased. We will check sources and stop if results are confusing. Health and emergency decisions go to qualified people. Any family member can ask us to pause.”

The details will differ from family to family. The value comes from making purpose, permissions, review, and stopping visible. An agent can take on useful work, while the family keeps the authority to decide what work is appropriate.
